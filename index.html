Published · eLife 2024 · featured in an eLife Insight

Endothelial regulatory genomics in atherosclerosis

15,220 nuclei204,904 chromatin peaks5 endothelial subtypes

Paired single-nucleus RNA and chromatin profiling of primary human aortic endothelial cells treated with IL1B, TGFB2 or ERG knockdown, integrated with single-cell data from human atherosclerotic lesions. Genetic risk variants for coronary artery disease were enriched in the open chromatin of one endothelial subtype, giving 81 candidate combinations of variant, regulatory element and target gene for experimental follow-up.

I conceived the study and built and ran the analysis pipeline on a university computing cluster. Data are public in GEO (GSE228428).

Do sex differences in heart and kidney disease begin in the capillaries?

138 hearts122 kidney participants432 GTEx hearts for replication

A replication-based single-cell analysis of a widely held idea: that the cells lining the smallest blood vessels differ between women and men. With the analysis plan fixed and time-stamped in advance, I found no sex difference in capillary gene programs, and could rule out large differences for most of them. What did replicate was in heart muscle cells, and in three X–Y gene pairs (KDM5C/KDM5D, KDM6A/UTY, USP9X/USP9Y) whose combined expression was lower in women.

How I work

  1. Start with the biological question

    Decide what would count as evidence before looking at the data. For the heart and kidney study, the questions, gene sets and statistical bounds were published in a time-stamped plan first.

  2. Use computation to narrow

    Integrate several human datasets instead of trusting one. In the eLife study, 15,220 nuclei, 204,904 chromatin peaks and a coronary disease genetic study reduced to 81 candidates.

  3. Try to break the result

    The donor is the unit of analysis; cells from one person are not independent observations. Shuffled-label tests and planted effects of known size show what each pipeline can detect, and second cohorts test what it found.

  4. Return to the biology

    Report what survived. The capillary difference was not there; the differences that replicated in 432 GTEx hearts were in heart muscle cells and in X–Y gene pairs.

Checking has caught real errors in my own analyses, including a pathology-note parser that counted “no atherosclerosis” as disease and a gene-set test that returned spurious pathways. Each is documented in the manuscript it affected.

Selected code

sex-difference-verify-agent

A small R agent built with ellmer and the Claude API. Claude selects and runs four tools (a sex-label check, a donor-level test, a permutation check and a planted-effect check) and calls a result a finding only if it passes. Run on simulated data and on 1,335 GTEx artery samples.

heart-capillary-sex-disease

Twenty R scripts and the time-stamped analysis plan for the single-cell study of sex differences in human heart and kidney.

Tools   R, Python, Bash, MATLAB · Seurat, Signac, chromVAR, Harmony, Cell Ranger ARC, demuxlet, DoubletFinder · edgeR, limma-voom · MAGMA, PLINK, samtools, VCFtools · ellmer · Linux and university HPC cluster

I use an AI assistant to help write analysis code. I design the analyses, run them, check the results and take responsibility for them.

About

My PhD, in clinical translational sciences with a bioinformatics dissertation, was done as the computational member of a wet lab at the University of Arizona. I analyzed the lab’s perturbation experiments, developed bench protocols for endothelial cell harvesting and imaging, and was principal investigator on an NIH F30 fellowship. I am now in the Physician Scientist Training Program in Internal Medicine at the University of Michigan.

From 2013 to 2018 I managed clinical trials at Stanford, including two multi-center randomized trials, and led development of a patient registry and its integration with electronic data capture. That is where I learned how clinical questions become datasets, and where those datasets break. After nearly two decades in translational research my questions are the same: which differences between people are real, which replicate and which matter for patients.
